From Gévaudan to Lozère

The history of morels
and Lozère

When an exceptional mushroom meets an exceptional land.

01

Lozère, a land born from Gévaudan

There are places where products are not simply harvested. They are sought, awaited, observed and earned. Lozère is one of them.

Before it was called Lozère, the region was historically associated with Gévaudan. The department was officially created in 1790, during the French Revolution, along the boundaries of the former province of Gévaudan.

The first inhabitants already lived from hunting and gathering. Dolmens, menhirs, caves and rock shelters bear witness to an ancient relationship between people and nature. Today, this history gives wild mushroom foraging its full meaning.

02

A land suited to mushrooms

Lozère is remarkably diverse in geographical and climatic terms. Aubrac, Margeride, the Causses, gorges and the Cévennes form a mosaic of landscapes.

With an average inhabited altitude close to 1,000 metres and forests covering around 45% of the department, the region offers a multitude of natural environments: pine trees, heathland, meadows, streams and rugged terrain.

This diversity partly explains the richness of local mycology: porcini, chanterelles, milk caps, trumpet chanterelles, black trumpets… and morels.

03

And then spring arrives…

The morel is not an ordinary mushroom. Rather, it announces the awakening of the forest, as nature gradually stirs again after the cold months.

Morels appear in spring, while many other forest species are more common in autumn. The season is short and conditions must be right.

And above all… you need to know where to look.

04

The treasure the forest does not reveal

Its honeycombed cap almost looks like a small natural sculpture. But its true distinction lies elsewhere: it is difficult to find.

You need to know the woods, observe the soil, understand the seasons, notice changes in vegetation and remember the places where nature has already offered something.

Sometimes you have to walk for a long time and find nothing. It is precisely this difficulty that gives the morel its value.

05

Knowledge passed down

Some foragers have known these woods for decades. They can recognize a type of ground, an exposure, a forest, a season and the famous mushroom spots.

This knowledge is often passed down discreetly. True expertise is not found only in the mushroom. It lies in the ability to find it.

“True expertise is not found only in the mushroom. It lies in the ability to find it.”
06

Why Lozère is so special

Lozère has vast, relatively preserved natural spaces. It is a sparsely populated land where forests occupy a considerable place and landscapes remain largely rural.

Forests, mountains, meadows, streams, granite, basalt, limestone and schist: several natural worlds meet here. This strong sense of place is especially important to the story of the morels.

07

Foraging, a patient gesture

In Lozère, searching for a morel takes time, attention and real knowledge of the land. The gesture may seem simple, but it is built on years of observation.

Respecting the undergrowth, not forcing nature and preserving gathering grounds are all part of this foraging culture.

08

The morel becomes a local product

A wild morel is not simply an agricultural product planted and mechanically harvested. You have to wait, observe, search, gather, sort, preserve and begin again the following year.

Some years are generous, others much less so. Weather conditions can change the growing periods and volumes.

The forest remains the master of the calendar.

09

From the forest to gastronomy

The aroma and texture of the morel make it a mushroom prized by gastronomy. In Lozère, the link between wild mushrooms and cuisine still lives on.

The product makes a true journey: the forest → the forager → the collector → the kitchen → the table.

A mushroom born in the undergrowth can find itself at the heart of a fine dish just a few days later.

10

A story that continues today

The tradition is not frozen in the past. Lozère businesses still work with the collection, sorting, preservation and processing of wild mushrooms.

Some work directly with forest owners and local foragers. An ancestral practice can thus become a genuine economic sector.

11

Morels and Gévaudan

A more recent story directly connects the morel to the name Gévaudan. In the 2010s, the Saugues area, located in the former historic territory of Gévaudan but now in Haute-Loire, sought to develop a sector around morel cultivation.

This distinction matters: Saugues is in Haute-Loire, not in the Lozère department. But this project shows the strength of the connection between the morel, the Massif Central and the identity of Gévaudan.

12

A land where nature is not forced

Wild morels cannot be ordered. They appear when nature brings together the right conditions. Each spring, foragers return to the woods, watch, search and hope.

Sometimes the forest is generous. Sometimes it keeps its secret. When they finally appear, morels represent a moment in the year, a territory and a body of knowledge.

13

From foraging to your table

Behind every product there may be a territory, a season, forests, foragers, hours of searching, expertise and careful selection.

The morel then becomes the final chapter of a story that began long before it reached your kitchen.
